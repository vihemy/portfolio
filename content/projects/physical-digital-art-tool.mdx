At **Trapholt** in Kolding, Denmark, an exhibition themed around the Danish concept of being a self-appointed judge of taste asks visitors to form and share opinions about art, then reflect on who they are and what they prefer. The platform I built ties together the devices the visitor touches into one continuous experience and harvests the research data the museum uses to study visitor behaviour.

## Challenge

The piece had to span four very different surfaces simultaneously, all bound to the same visit:

- **Custom handheld devices** ("TID") — bespoke NFC-scanning hardware visitors carry through the gallery to "collect" artworks.
- **Touchscreen kiosks** ("stations") — group registration, artwork browsing, surveys, email opt-in.
- **Visitors' own smartphones** — joined via a QR code from a kiosk session, with the same visit GUID persisting across devices.
- **Large wall displays** — live anonymized aggregate visualizations of visitor choices.

None of those is a browser tab on a developer's laptop. The handhelds emit noisy JSON over MQTT. The kiosks are touched all day by guests who've never seen them. The phones are running whatever the visitor brought. The wall displays are unattended. And under all of it sits the museum's research question: *what do visitors actually do here, and what does that tell us about taste?*

The previous flow at the museum implied spreadsheets hand-rolled from logs and an exhibition that didn't really know its own audience. The brief was to build the platform — and to build it so the museum's research team could use the data responsibly.

## Approach

I treated the system as **a stateful backend with five unequal clients**, and engineered the edges so each client could be sloppy without breaking the centre.

### One database, one source of truth

A single **SQLite** database (`better-sqlite3`) holds everything. Migrations are sequential, hand-written SQL. Repositories (`db/repositories/...`) are the only thing that touches tables — every consumer — every service worker, every SvelteKit route, every stats endpoint — goes through the repository layer. Versioning is meaningful: `surveys` are `PRIMARY KEY (sanity_id, version)` with a `latest_surveys` SQL view, so the museum can edit a survey in Sanity while responses to the previous version stay analyzable.

### Fault-tolerant ingestion at theedges

The handhelds emit JSON over MQTT, and their payloads are not pristine. The MQTT ingestion service parses in three escalating passes — direct `JSON.parse`, regex repair, then a pattern-extraction fallback — and isolates every interaction so a single malformed dump never poisons the batch. Every raw payload is also written to a `device_dumps` audit table; telemetry (voltage, temperature, humidity, pressure) is validated with **DB-level CHECK constraints that enforce units** (mV/V, °C/F/K/R, %/RH, hPa/mb), so a sensor reporting nonsense is rejected at the storage layer rather than three weeks later in someone's analysis.

### Svelte 5 on the kiosks, SSE-synced multi-surface UX

Each kiosk is a Svelte 5 SPA flow inside the SvelteKit app — group registration (controller + presenter screens live-synced), artwork browser with images + bar charts + QR link-out to mobile, surveys, AI reflection, email opt-in, exit screen. An **SSE broadcast hub** handles the live-sync: any screen `POST /api/events/entry`, every open stream on `/api/events/regurgitate` receives it. The wall display integrates with **Pixilab Blocks** (`@nopa/svelte-plus-blocks`) so the museum can programmatically switch it between an artwork-stats block and a group-presenter block. A custom on-screen keyboard and number pad (`simple-keyboard`) handles touchscreen input; a central localisation store keeps everything bilingual (`da` / `en`).

### Survey-driven segmentation

Four survey types are routed from the CMS — **demographics**, **segmentation**, **intervention** (an AI reflection prompt), and **reflection** — with question types ranging from single/multiple choice and rating scales to thumbs-up, free text, and postal code. Segmentation scoring assigns every visitor an archetype ("knowledge seeker", "recharger", "experience collector", "professionally interested", "social visitor"). The wall display rotates between artwork favourites, segment breakdowns (by gender, age group, region — Zealand vs Jutland/Funen via postal-code mapping), and idle states.

### Privacy-engineered exports

The research tail is the reason the platform exists, and the export pipeline is where the privacy engineering earns its keep:

- **Emails are hashed before any export** — the workbook the museum opens never contains a raw address.
- **Postal codes are mapped to Danish regions** with a deduplication pass — the data set is useful to research, useless to a doxxer.
- A **log-obscuring script** back-fills historical emails so old logs can be analyzed without leaking anything the policy has since tightened on.
- A **Power BI-ready REST endpoint** (`GET /api/export/all`) plus per-table JSON, a schema-introspection endpoint, and per-table CSV exports give the research team their pick of tools; a nested-JSON-flattener script handles the awkward ones.

### Ops

Everything runs under **PM2** with an ecosystem config, `pm2 reload` / `start` / `stop` npm scripts, `pm2-logrotate` (10 MB cap, debug-ping heartbeats suppressed so the logs are actually readable), `db:migrate` / `db:seed` / `db:reset` scripts, and JSON seed files for all four survey definitions. Every integration — MQTT broker, Sanity, the museum's collection-management API, Google Sheets, Mailchimp/Mandrill, OpenAI — is configured via `.env` with a checked-in `.env.example`. Unit tests cover the survey-response processing pipeline (formatting + segment calculation).

## Outcomes

The museum's research team now pulls privacy-safe exports straight from the platform instead of hand-editing spreadsheets from logs. Visitors get a continuous experience across a device they carry, a kiosk they touch, a phone they already own, and a wall they glance at — and every artefact of that experience lands in one versioned, type-checked database under their feet. For me the lasting lesson is that **fault tolerance lives at the edges, and correctness lives at the centre**: the handhelds and the kiosks are forgiven every kind of sloppiness, and the storage layer is merciless.
